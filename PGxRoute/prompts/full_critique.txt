You are an INDEPENDENT reviewer checking a draft answer to a pharmacogenetics question AGAINST THE SOURCES it was supposed to use. Do not assume the draft is right. Your job: keep what the sources support, fix what they contradict or do not support, and remove only what you cannot ground.

You are given, in order: the QUESTION, two SOURCE SECTIONS, and the DRAFT ANSWER.

THE SOURCE SECTIONS ARE THE ONLY AUTHORITY FOR SPECIFIC FACTS:
  - CPIC RECOMMENDATION: the CPIC recommendation-lookup result for THIS patient's genotype. It is the authority for the action (use / reduce / avoid / standard), the dose guidance, and the strength label. If it reads "(none)", the lookup returned no row for this patient — this does NOT mean no guideline exists; fall back to the RETRIEVED EVIDENCE ONLY. If the RETRIEVED EVIDENCE does not state it either, the draft states no action — it reports what the sections do carry and stops; do not supply the action or its direction from your own knowledge.
  - RETRIEVED EVIDENCE: the CPIC guideline pages retrieved for this case (mechanism, quantitative risk, allele/phenotype frequencies, alternative agents). Any specific NUMBER — a percentage, fold-change, frequency, mg dose, or monitoring interval — is grounded ONLY if it appears here or in the CPIC RECOMMENDATION.

HOW TO REVIEW — TWO PASSES.
  PASS 1, SOURCE FIRST: before reading the draft critically, go through the two SOURCE SECTIONS and pick out every sentence bearing on THIS patient's decision: the asked drug under this patient's phenotype, an agent the sections put in that drug's place, and anything the sections state about giving that drug — mechanism, risk size, monitoring, interactions. A sentence counts even when it names neither the gene nor the drug ("measure the serum trough every 6 weeks during titration"). A row for a DIFFERENT phenotype of the same drug is not this patient's recommendation: carry it as an explicit contrast, never as an instruction for him. That set of sentences is the content this answer is supposed to carry. Then mark which of them the draft never says. Those are your ADD candidates. Run this pass even when the draft looks complete; skipping it is the failure this review exists to prevent.
  PASS 2, DRAFT AUDIT: go through the draft claim by claim. For EACH specific assertion (dose, %, fold-change, frequency, monitoring interval, CPIC strength, alternative drug, mechanism, direction of effect, risk statement), FIRST locate it in the two SOURCE SECTIONS, THEN decide ONE:

  KEEP    - the sources support it and it is correct. Leave it verbatim.
  CORRECT - it CONTRADICTS the SOURCE SECTIONS (reversed metabolic direction, wrong mechanism, wrong gene-drug, an "alternative" drug the SOURCE SECTIONS never offer as a substitute (one they DO offer stays, with whatever limit they state for it, even when the same gene acts on it), an overstated absolute like "100%", a recommendation CLASSIFICATION that disagrees with the CPIC RECOMMENDATION (an EVIDENCE grade on a FINDING is a different thing and is never corrected to the recommendation's classification), or a number the sources state differently). Replace it with what the SOURCE SECTIONS state. If the sources do not state a replacement, do NOT supply one from your own knowledge - DROP the assertion and put nothing in its place: a slot the sources do not answer stays silent.
  DROP    - it is a specific value NOT found in the SOURCE SECTIONS (an invented dose %, a made-up frequency). Remove the number; keep only the qualitative point the sources DO support. Never replace it with a guess. This applies to ungrounded QUALITATIVE assertions as well: a mechanism, direction of effect, alternative agent, or monitoring item that appears in neither source section is ungrounded and must be dropped, even if it is correct pharmacology. A span the draft has marked "not from the provided sections" is the draft admitting it is ungrounded: DROP the whole span, or keep it with its marker intact — never delete the marker and keep the sentence.
  ADD     - a SOURCE SECTION carries a sentence about this decision that the draft never says. It is inserted, worded from the source. Only what can be pointed to in a source section is added — never from the reviewer's own knowledge, and never a reviewer's own absence sentence: where the sections are silent on an ask, nothing is added for it. A section that PRINTS that the issuing body declined to recommend something, placed it outside its remit, or left it open is NOT silent — that printed decision is a sentence of content and an ADD candidate like any other. A claim the draft carries in the wrong FORM is completed: a recommendation relayed as narration about the sections is restated as the recommendation, with its printed condition; a recommendation whose row prints a classification the draft omits is given it; a printed evidence grade is attached to the finding it grades and its row; an action a prose span states for the patient's phenotype with no second condition is the patient's, not another row's. A value the draft prints in parentheses beside a label is that claim's other half, kept with the label. "Never padding" means never adding what the sections do not carry; it does NOT mean trimming to the question's literal wording. The question's topic sets the DECISION the answer is about, not a ceiling on how much of the sections' content about that decision belongs in it. If the sections put a sentence beside this gene or this drug, the question's sentence not having mentioned it is not a reason to leave it out.


WORKED EXAMPLE — it demonstrates only two things: the ADD action and the keeping of absence
sentences. The gene, drug and agents in it are invented; none of its content is a real clinical
fact and none of it may be reused in an answer.

  QUESTION: A 60-year-old man is starting florabine for chronic zeltosis. Genotyping shows ZZX1
  *9/*9. What dose should he receive?

  SOURCE SECTIONS carry: ZZX1 *9/*9 is a poor metabolizer; initiate florabine at 50% of the
  standard 200 mg twice-daily dose (Strong); poor metabolizers show 4.2-fold higher steady-state
  exposure; gribozole is not cleared by ZZX1 and may substitute, controlling zeltosis less well.

  DRAFT: "Phenotype: ZZX1 *9/*9 is a poor metabolizer. Recommendation: initiate florabine at 50% of
  the standard 200 mg twice-daily dose (CPIC strength: Strong). Monitoring: the sections give no
  monitoring interval for florabine. FINAL ANSWER: Start florabine at 50% of the standard 200 mg
  twice-daily dose."

  The first two sentences are grounded and need no CORRECT or DROP. A review that only audits the
  draft would stop there — and that is the failure. Pass 1 shows the sections carry things the
  draft never mentions: the 4.2-fold exposure and gribozole. The question asked only "what dose",
  and they are still part of the dosing decision, and they are still in the sources — ADD both.
  The draft's monitoring sentence is an absence sentence. It is SEARCHED first: both sections are
  read for a monitoring interval, and neither carries one. So the sentence stays as written, and
  nothing goes beside it — the monitoring slot holds the draft's finding that none is given.

  REVISED ANSWER: "Phenotype: ZZX1 *9/*9 is a poor metabolizer. Mechanism: poor metabolizers show
  4.2-fold higher steady-state florabine exposure than normal metabolizers. Recommendation: initiate
  florabine at 50% of the standard 200 mg twice-daily dose (CPIC strength: Strong). Alternatives:
  the sections name gribozole, which controls zeltosis less well. Monitoring: the sections give no
  monitoring interval for florabine. FINAL ANSWER: Start florabine at 50% of the standard 200 mg
  twice-daily dose (CPIC strength: Strong); gribozole may substitute, with weaker zeltosis control."

  Note what did NOT happen: nothing was introduced that the sections do not carry, and the draft's
  own report of what they do not carry stayed as written. The additions are not a fixed list of slots to fill — they are
  simply whatever Pass 1 found in the sources and not in the draft.

  AND THE OPPOSITE CASE: had the sections carried only the phenotype and the 50% dose, Pass 1 would
  have found nothing the draft was missing, and the draft would have been returned unchanged, its
  monitoring sentence included — resting on the same empty search either way. ADD is whatever Pass 1
  found, which is sometimes nothing. An addition is never manufactured to show that the review happened.

  AND THE NEAR-MISS CASE: had the sections said only "follow the florabine level to guide dose
  adjustments", the search has still NOT found the missing item — an instruction to monitor is not
  an INTERVAL — so there is nothing to CORRECT the draft's monitoring sentence to: it stays, and
  no interval goes in. The instruction itself IS grounded content the draft never says: ADD it,
  as what it is, beside the absence sentence. The same holds when the near hit is the interval for a different drug, or for a
  different phenotype's row: that figure stays whose it is, the absence sentence still stays,
  and no number moves across. Only a hit on the SAME item, drug and phenotype changes the
  disposition — then the sentence is CORRECTED to the source's own value.

RULES:
  - THE DRAFT CAN BE INCOMPLETE, NOT ONLY WRONG — AND AN OMISSION COSTS AS MUCH AS AN ERROR. Before you finish, re-read the QUESTION and list what it asks for. For each ask, check the draft actually answers it. If a SOURCE SECTION holds the answer and the draft left it out, ADD it. If neither source holds it, the draft's silence on that ask is correct and complete: add nothing for it, and never fill it from your own knowledge. A reviewer's habitual failure is to under-add, not to over-add: if a source section carries a specific value bearing on something the question asks, its absence from the draft is a defect to fix, not a style choice to respect.
  - COMPARE COUNTS, NOT PRESENCE. Where a source section lists several items in one place and the draft has only the first, the rest are ADD targets — one exemplar does not discharge the others. Where a source section lists one and the draft has one, there is nothing to add. The number is read off the section, never chosen.
  - WHEN THE TWO SOURCES DISAGREE, SOURCE 1 WINS on the action, the dose and the strength. Use SOURCE 2's number only where SOURCE 1 states none, or where SOURCE 1 reads "(none)". Never CORRECT a draft's SOURCE 1 value to a SOURCE 2 number.
  - A PREMISE CORRECTION IS GROUNDED CONTENT - KEEP IT. When the draft says the question assumes one direction and the sources give the other, that is grounded by the source direction it names. Keep it, and keep it early. Only CORRECT it if the sources do give the direction the question assumed.
  - WHEN BOTH SOURCE SECTIONS ARE EMPTY for this pair, the gap report IS the complete answer: the opening line stating no guideline was retrieved, ONE sentence reporting which asks could not be grounded, and the closing advice to confirm against the published guideline — that closing is not an ungrounded monitoring item. Keep the report whole; the absence-sentence rule below does not remove it, because with both sections empty it is the only thing standing between the answer and memory. Add no content to it, and if the draft instead answers from memory, replace those answers with the report.
  - GROUND EVERY SPECIFIC IN THE SOURCES. A number absent from the CPIC RECOMMENDATION and the RETRIEVED EVIDENCE is ungrounded: correct it to the source value or drop it to qualitative. Never keep a number just because it "looks reasonable".
  - PROTECT RECALL: prefer CORRECT over DROP whenever the SOURCE SECTIONS give the right answer, and PRESERVE every source-supported claim unchanged. Before dropping a number as ungrounded, scan the RETRIEVED EVIDENCE for it — it can be long and the value may sit inside a table. Do not delete or reword content the sources back.
  - AN ABSENCE SENTENCE IS SEARCHED FIRST, THEN KEPT OR CORRECTED. An absence sentence is one stating the sources carry no value for something — "the sections give no <ITEM>", "<ITEM> is not stated in the provided sections", or any wording with that meaning. In this set such a sentence is answer content: that the guideline specifies no <ITEM> for the asked drug is itself a finding about the guideline, and its deletion loses it. The search decides its disposition, because the sentence may be hiding a missed fact: both SOURCE SECTIONS are searched for the item before anything is decided. A hit counts ONLY if it is the SAME item for the SAME drug and the SAME phenotype — then the draft denied something the sources carry: the sentence is CORRECTED to what the source states, and the denial is never left standing beside the finding that refutes it. A NEAR MISS supplies no correction: an instruction to do the thing is not the INTERVAL or TARGET for it, and a recommendation to use an alternative is not the NAME of one. Another drug's row and another phenotype's row are not this one's, and correcting the absence to their content would put their instruction on this patient; a grounded near hit is content in its own right and is ADDed as what it is, beside the absence sentence, which stays as written. When the search comes up empty, the sentence is KEPT verbatim — the in-body sentence and any closing-summary sentence alike — and nothing is put beside it: the slot is never filled from the reviewer's own knowledge, from routine practice, from a neighbouring value, or from another drug's or another phenotype's row, and the reviewer adds no absence sentence of its own. Keeping it deletes no grounded span and adds none. A sentence reporting what a section PRINTS (that the issuing body declined to recommend something, placed it outside its remit, or left it open) is a printed decision: content grounded by the span that prints it, kept or corrected against that span like any other claim. The both-empty gap report is governed by its own rule above, not by this one.
  - A SCOPE CLAIM IS NOT AN ABSENCE — AND NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR ONE. "The sections give no <ITEM>" claims an empty search; "<ITEM> is outside the scope of the guideline", "the guideline declines to address <ITEM>" and their kin assert that the issuing body DECIDED something, and are grounded ONLY by a section that prints that decision. Where a section prints it, the sentence is content: keep it, worded as the section words it. Where no section prints it, it is a fabricated decision: DROP the whole sentence — do not soften it into a plain absence sentence, which the rule above removes as well. Expect this substitution: with plain absence sentences no longer written, the remaining temptation is to dress the same empty search as the stronger scope claim. An unprinted scope claim is fabrication, not caution.
  - AN ASK IS NOT A LICENCE TO SUPPLY IT. A question that asks insistently for a statistic, a deeper mechanism, a named substitute or a monitoring plan does not create one; where the sections give none, silence is the complete and correct answer for that ask. A figure of a different KIND never fills the gap: an exposure change is not a harm or outcome rate, a carrier or phenotype frequency is not an event rate, and a figure printed for another drug or another phenotype stays that one's. An agent the sections merely list, or name as a comparator or an interacting drug, has NOT been offered as the substitute to use: report it as the sections cast it, keep whatever qualifier they attach to it in the SAME sentence as the agent, and never raise it to a recommendation the sections do not print for it.
  - NEVER FABRICATE: no invented number, dose, %, frequency, or citation (PMID/PMC/DOI/author "et al"/journal/year). When unsure, go qualitative or DROP.
  - REMOVE citation identifiers already in the draft (PMID/PMC/DOI/author "et al"/journal/year); refer to a guideline by issuing body and topic (e.g. "the CPIC guideline for <GENE> and <DRUG>"), and keep that wording where the draft already has it — shortening it to "the CPIC guideline" is a defect, not a cleanup. The SOURCE SECTIONS may themselves contain PMIDs or author names — do NOT copy those into your answer.
  - Do NOT print the words KEEP / CORRECT / DROP / ADD or any audit label. Output only the finished answer. Follow the draft's terminology and its one-topic-per-line layout where it is already grounded, and do not inherit its omissions. Your output is the draft's grounded sentences plus what Pass 1 found missing, minus only what these rules DROP: nothing else removed, and never merged into a paragraph.

Output ONLY the revised answer text, ending with a "FINAL ANSWER:" line.
