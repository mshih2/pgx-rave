## Construction principles

**A question** states the gene, the genotype (as a test report gives it), the drug, the
population and the clinical context. Every detail in it has a job: it makes a printed condition,
row or caveat apply or not apply. A question asks for two or three things, not five.

**An answer** follows the format used in clinical pharmacogenomic practice, modelled on the
pharmacist example pairs in what it covers, its format and its easy-to-digest style: the
recommendation and what it rests on first, then the caveats and monitoring a clinician acts on.
Every sentence carries a fact a clinician would act on or check.

**Grounding.** A PGx answer is built only from its tier's CPIC source: an intermediate answer from
the CPIC recommendation lookup, a hard answer from the lookup plus the CPIC guideline and
supplement passages given. Not from other guidelines, trials, product labels or the writer's own
pharmacogenomic knowledge. Where the CPIC sources given do not address an ask, the answer says so
plainly, once, without inventing a reason; a reason or a decision is reported only where CPIC
prints it.

**Fidelity.**
- The lookup is authoritative for the phenotype, the recommendation, its action and its
  strength; passages explain and quantify it and never change it. Where the lookup and a
  guideline passage differ, the lookup is current and the passage is a snapshot of its
  publication date; say which is which.
- Quote a number only where the source prints it; do not convert units or carry a figure from
  one drug, phenotype, population or column to another.
- Keep the source's modality ("may", "consider", "only"). Firming a hedge changes the content.
- A printed list of options keeps CPIC's printing verb ("CPIC names X, Y and Z as
  alternatives"), so it is not read as an order to use each one.
- Name the kind of document a fact comes from (the CPIC recommendation lookup, the guideline
  text, a named table, the supplement). No PMIDs, DOIs or record numbers.

**Common questions** are everyday medication questions with no PGx content. Their answers are
short, practical and correct for a general adult patient.
